## Answers to the 5 review questions

### Q1. ADR-0001: demotion or hard implementation?

**Demotion is the correct call for v0.1.**

The ADR is well-structured: clear context, explicit decision, consequences with accepted
costs and gains, alternatives table, and a well-defined revisit-trigger. The argument is
sound:

- Implementing §6.6 requires a **schema migration** (adding machine-readable acceptance
  ids to the card format) plus a new `--task-card` parameter to gate.py plus updating
  every template, example, and existing verdict. That's a version bump, not a fix.
- A "spec promise with no code behind it" is worse than a "documented gap." The ADR
  frames this correctly: "a specification that promises more than the checker delivers
  is a marketing document."
- The revisit-trigger (L2 team reports skipped criteria, 3+ adopters request it, or
  schema changes for another reason) gives a clear path back without the v0.1 schema
  migration.

No further action needed.

### Q2. `--evidence-must-match` off by default: honest trade or comfortable denial?

**Honest trade. I cannot argue against it effectively.**

The reasoning chain is:
1. A **default** regex is arbitrary — what pattern do you ship? `exit [0-9]|passed|failed`
   is suggested but why not `row count == N`? The gate can't know your domain's
   evidence vocabulary.
2. A default that's too **strict** rejects honest short evidence ("tests pass" is valid).
3. A default that's too **weak** is just a rubber stamp with extra steps.
4. Therefore: no default is better than a wrong default. Opt-in forces the team to
   think about their own evidence bar.

The "comfortable denial" risk is real: teams that never opt in get zero protection.
But that's the nature of a **floor** — it sets a minimum, and the minimum here is
"evidence is a non-empty list of strings." The gap between "non-empty" and "actually
verified" is a quality issue, not a gate issue. The gate can't know what "good"
evidence looks like for your domain. §10.6 documents this honestly.

One suggestion: consider a **warning** (not error) when evidence entries are all
< 8 characters. This flags `["ok"]` without blocking honest short evidence. Not
required for this round.

### Q3. FUTURE_VERDICT 300s skew: exploitable?

**No meaningful exploitation path.**

Analysis:
- **Forward-date (future ts):** A reviewer sets `ts = now + 299s`. The gate passes
  (within 300s skew). But after 300s, the ts is no longer in the future — it becomes
  a normal past timestamp. Any artifact modified after that ts will trigger STALE.
  The reviewer has gained at most 300s of grace. Not a durable exploit.
- **Backdate (past ts):** A reviewer sets `ts = now - 3600s`. The verdict is
  immediately stale for any artifact modified in the last hour. Self-defeating.
- **300s default:** Covers NTP drift (typically < 100ms) and reasonable clock
  inaccuracy. Not large enough for meaningful exploitation. 300s is fine.

The one legitimate concern: if the **gate and the file system use different clocks**
(e.g., gate runs on a CI server, artifacts are on a different machine), 300s might
not be enough. The `--max-clock-skew` flag handles this — teams with cross-machine
setups can raise it. Documented. No issue.

### Q4. OWNED_FILES_CONFLICT as WARN: correct?

**Correct as shipped. The design already handles the L2 case.**

The severity philosophy in `docs/lint-rules.md` says:
> ERROR means "the protocol cannot be evaluated here" — a machine reading this
> directory would be misled. WARN means "this smells" and a human may have a good reason.

Two active cards claiming the same path **does** mislead a machine — so by this
philosophy it *could* be ERROR. But the counter-argument:

- At **L1** (human relay), two agents declaring overlapping ownership might be
  mid-reassignment. A human arbitrator can see both cards and resolve it. WARN
  is the right signal: "here's a conflict, human, please look."
- At **L2** (CI, no human), `--strict` promotes all WARNs to ERRORs. So in
  production, OWNED_FILES_CONFLICT **is** an ERROR. The conformance level
  system already encodes the right severity per deployment.

The design is: WARN by default (L1), ERROR under `--strict` (L2). This is correct.
The task card's instinct ("data loss, not style") is right about the consequence,
but the mechanism already delivers the right severity at the right conformance level.

One suggestion: document in `docs/lint-rules.md` that OWNED_FILES_CONFLICT is
"recommended to be ERROR in L2 deployments" — making the --strict dependency
explicit rather than leaving it implicit.

### Q5. Rule count vs. reading willingness

**Not at risk yet. No cuts recommended.**

- 9 postmortems each map to a specific rule or limitation. Removing one orphans
  its rule — you'd have a rule with no "why," which is exactly the anti-pattern
  this project exists to prevent.
- The conformance level system (L0/L1/L2) is the reading filter:
  - L0: ignore everything.
  - L1: read PROTOCOL.md §1–§7 + lint-rules.md. Postmortems optional.
  - L2: read everything. All 9 PMs + 8 gate checks + 10 lint rules.
- 8 gate checks is a reasonable number for a CI job — each one is one line of
  config. 10 lint rules is fine — the linter is the one that reads them, not humans.

The risk is not "too many rules" — it's "no one reads them." That's solved by
the tiered conformance model. If a team is at L2 and still not reading, no
number of rules (or cuts) will help.
